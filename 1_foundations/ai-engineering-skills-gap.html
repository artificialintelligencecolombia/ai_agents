Skills taxonomy · repo audit

The AI engineering skills map

What the industry expects from an AI engineer in 2026, organized into 14 domains — then checked line by line against six weeks of Master AI Agentic Engineering: what's taught in the core curriculum, what only shows up in student community contributions, and what's missing entirely.

Method: the taxonomy comes from ~15 targeted searches across 2026 AI-engineer job postings, roadmap sites, and framework/tooling comparisons (LangChain, CrewAI, AutoGen, MCP, RAG eval, observability, fine-tuning). The repo side comes from a full scan of the root dependency manifests, every notebook and script across 1_foundations → 6_mcp, guides/, 99_bonus_material/ — plus a sweep of roughly 800 student community_contributions/ submissions.

final answer

The 12 gaps worth closing first

Ranked by how often 2026 job postings and roadmaps ask for them, weighed against how absent they are from this repo.

  1. RAG as a first-class discipline

    Vector-DB selection, chunking, hybrid search, and reranking are the single most-deployed production pattern industry-wide — here, RAG is invisible plumbing inside CrewAI's memory system, never its own lesson.

  2. Evaluation frameworks

    RAGAS, DeepEval, and golden-dataset methodology show up in nearly every senior AI-engineer job description. The course has the right instinct (LLM-as-judge, evaluator nodes) but no tooling to operationalize it.

  3. Fine-tuning & PEFT

    LoRA, QLoRA, RLHF, and DPO are entirely absent — the course is 100% prompting and orchestration, with no path from "prompt isn't working" to "fine-tune a small model."

  4. Math & ML theory foundations

    Zero linear algebra, probability/statistics, or transformer-internals content. Fine for using APIs, but a ceiling for anyone who needs to reason about model behavior or costs.

  5. Production observability stack

    OpenAI native traces and LangSmith are covered, but Langfuse/Helicone-style cost tracking, latency budgets, and eval-in-CI — the tools that turn a demo into an on-call-able service — aren't.

  6. Security as a discipline

    Guardrails exist as an implementation pattern, but prompt-injection defense and the OWASP LLM Top 10 are never framed as their own curriculum, despite being the highest-severity risk category in production agents.

  7. Deployment/MLOps maturity

    Gradio + Hugging Face Spaces is a genuinely real deployment path, but FastAPI, CI/CD, cloud-native serving, and experiment tracking — the layer between "it runs" and "it's on-call" — are missing.

  8. Context engineering & prompt caching

    Called the direct successor to prompt engineering by multiple 2026 sources; the course does the work implicitly (managing state, tool outputs) but never names or teaches it as a skill.

  9. No-code workflow automation

    n8n and Zapier are name-dropped in a few student reflections but never actually built — a real gap given how much production "agentic automation" hiring now happens on these platforms.

  10. Voice & multimodal agents

    No Whisper, Realtime API, or voice-agent coverage; multimodal is touched only lightly in one AutoGen notebook.

  11. Newer inter-agent standards

    MCP is covered in unusual depth for a public course, but the adjacent A2A (Agent2Agent) protocol and Anthropic's own Claude Agent SDK aren't used anywhere.

  12. TypeScript/JS and formal SQL

    Node is only ever used to launch MCP servers, never taught as a language; SQL is used operationally (SQLite) throughout but never taught as a querying skill.

Where the repo is ahead of the curve

  • MCP depth — a full week building both MCP servers and clients from scratch is rare; most courses treat MCP as a footnote.
  • Framework breadth — five production agent frameworks (OpenAI Agents SDK, CrewAI, LangChain/LangGraph, AutoGen) in six weeks is unusually wide literacy.
  • Hands-on multi-agent patterns — handoffs, guardrails, structured outputs, and orchestrator–worker are all built, not just described.
  • Real deployment — shipping to Hugging Face Spaces beats the "notebook-only" ceiling most courses stop at.
